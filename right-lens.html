Choosing the right lens

You know your camera's sensor and you know how much of the sample you want to see. Which lens magnification do you need — and what's the smallest detail you'll be able to see? Six short steps, each with a model to play with.

How big is the sensor?

The sensor is a grid of tiny light-sensitive squares (pixels). Its physical size is just number of pixels × pixel size.

Our camera: 640 × 512 pixels, each 15 µm × 15 µm. That makes a chip only 9.6 mm × 7.68 mm — smaller than a fingernail.

In plain words: count the tiles along one side and multiply by the tile width.

Squeeze the field of view onto the sensor

We want to see 27 mm of the sample across, but the sensor is only 9.6 mm wide. The lens must shrink the scene so it just fits.

That shrink factor is the primary magnification (PMAG): sensor size ÷ field of view. Here 9.6 ÷ 27 ≈ 0.36×, so we pick a 0.36× lens.

The smallest pattern a sensor can see

Resolution is measured with a pattern of line pairs: one dark line plus one bright line. To tell dark from bright you need at least one pixel for each, so the finest pattern the sensor can record is one line pair per two pixels.

With 15 µm pixels a line pair is 30 µm wide, so the sensor can resolve 1000 ÷ 30 ≈ 33.3 line pairs per mm (image space).

Shrink the pattern below 2 pixels per pair and watch the recorded signal turn into mush. Even at exactly 2 it only works when the bars line up with the pixels — that's why 2 is the absolute limit.

From the sensor back to the sample

The lens shrinks everything by PMAG. So a pattern that is just resolvable on the sensor is bigger on the sample by 1 ÷ PMAG.

In line pairs per mm, multiply: 0.36 × 33.3 ≈ 12 lp/mm on the sample. One line pair is then 1000 ÷ 12 ≈ 83 µm, and one line — the smallest detail — is half of that: about 42 µm.

That's simply pixel size ÷ PMAG = 15 ÷ 0.36 ≈ 42 µm: one pixel's footprint on the sample.

Keep in mind

  • This is the sensor's limit, not the whole system's. The lens itself must also resolve at least 33 lp/mm at the sensor (check its MTF or resolution spec), or it, not the pixels, sets the limit.
  • 2 pixels per line pair is the best case. It only works when the bars line up with the pixels (step 3). In practice, plan for a bit more, e.g. 2.5–3 pixels per smallest feature, if you need it crisp.
  • Same number, two names: the "object-side pixel size" used in the heat calculation (σ = (40 µm)²) is this same 15 µm ÷ 0.36 ≈ 42 µm.
